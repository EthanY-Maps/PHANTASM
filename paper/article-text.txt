Abstract
Historic street maps record urban development and the growth of transport networks, but they survive as raster images with no spatial reference and substantial noise. This paper presents a pipeline that converts historic raster maps into georeferenced road graphs and matches them against OpenStreetMap. Its five stages segment roads with a U-Net, convert the predictions into a topologically consistent graph, read and propagate street names with optical character recognition, geolocate the map by matching recognised names to modern intersections, and match historic links to modern links by geometric similarity. The pipeline is evaluated on the John Sands maps of Sydney (1877–1903), twelve sheets of the 1886 Atlas of the Suburbs of Sydney, and a held-out 1942 map of Melbourne. Segmentation reaches a Dice coefficient of 0.856 on the unseen 1903 sheet and 0.840 on Melbourne, and graph construction reaches F1 of 0.829 to 0.882 on the held-out sheets. Fully automated geolocation succeeds only on the 1903 sheet; a semi-automatic mode using manually placed control points extends the evaluation to fourteen maps, with median residuals of 4.3 to 30 m. Matching reaches F1 of 0.573 to 0.724 on the atlas sheets and 0.651 on the 1903 sheet. The pipeline matches 97.4% of the historic links that have a modern road within 30 m, so the matching scores depend mainly on how much of each historic network has a modern counterpart.

1. Introduction
Historic street maps preserve valuable information on street layouts, transport networks, and urban form, but they remain difficult to use quantitatively because most survive only as scanned raster images without explicit spatial reference. Extracting road networks from such maps at scale is challenging because of degradation, stylistic variation, dense annotation, and hand-drawn geometric distortion. Traditional digitisation relies on manual tracing and georeferencing, which is slow and difficult to scale. We refer to this condition, in which a raster map contains rich spatial information but no direct coordinate system or network representation, as the “blind map” problem.
Recent deep learning methods, particularly U-Net-style architectures (Ronneberger et al., 2015) and later cartographic extensions (Patel et al., 2022), have improved automated road extraction from historic maps. However, these methods usually produce segmentation masks rather than the graph representations needed for network analysis, and address only part of the workflow, such as segmentation, text extraction, or georeferencing in isolation.
This paper presents PHANTASM, an end-to-end pipeline for historic street map digitisation. The pipeline:
1.
extracts road networks from raster historic maps using a U-Net segmentation model;
2.
converts binary road predictions into graph representations for transport network analysis;
3.
extracts and propagates street-name information using OCR and spatial reasoning;
4.
performs automated geolocation through semantic matching of historic intersection names to contemporary OSM data; and
5.
supports quantitative comparison between reconstructed historic networks and modern road systems.
Segmentation is evaluated on five held-out maps and graph construction on four, across three map series and two cities. Fully automated geolocation is demonstrated on one map, the 1903 John Sands sheet, which is the only sheet with enough validated OCR anchors, so that result is a demonstration of feasibility rather than evidence of generalisation. Stages 4–5 are evaluated on fourteen maps using the semi-automatic geolocation mode of Section 5.4.8, in which an operator places a small number of control points that the system extends by junction matching.

3. Related work
3.1. Deep learning for historic road extraction
Advances in computer vision have enabled progress in automated road extraction from historic maps. Deep learning architectures, particularly encoder–decoder networks, are now a common approach for pixel-level road segmentation from cartographic imagery. Broad reviews situate these developments within a wider methodological landscape: Jiao et al. (2021) synthesise road-feature extraction methods from raster maps across traditional and deep learning paradigms, while Chiang et al. (2020) outline the broader scientific challenges and best practices associated with the use of historic maps.
Within this literature, multi-class road segmentation on historic cartography has shown clear gains from modern deep architectures, but also clear sensitivity to class imbalance and map style. On the Deutsche Heereskarte 1:200,000 Türkei (DHK 200 Turkey) maps, the strongest configuration reported by Avcı et al. (2022) combined a split-attention encoder (Timm resnest200e) with U-Net++, yielding 98.99% overall accuracy, 41.99% IoU, 51.41% precision, and 69.7% recall across five road classes. The gap between overall accuracy and the class-specific metrics underscores a recurring issue in historic-map segmentation: easy background pixels can dominate aggregate performance even when the road predictions themselves remain imperfect.
Attention-enhanced encoder–decoder models have also improved road segmentation in remote sensing more generally. For example, the CNN-transformer U-Net variants examined by Wang et al. (2023) illustrate the value of attention mechanisms for extracting elongated transport features. However, the underlying imagery in that literature is contemporary satellite data rather than degraded historic cartography, so the reported gains cannot be transferred directly without accounting for symbology, annotation density, and physical map deterioration.
The literature also suggests that intersection extraction should be treated as a distinct task rather than a simple by-product of road segmentation. On USGS historical topographic maps, Saeedimoghaddam and Stepinski (2020) framed intersection detection as an object-detection problem using a region-based CNN with an Inception-ResNet v2 encoder, reporting F1 scores of 0.80 and 0.86 for single- and double-line road symbols respectively. That work further showed that edge density, colour complexity, and image sharpness materially affect extraction quality, reinforcing the need for a separate intersection-identification stage and motivating the name-based anchoring strategy adopted in Stage 4 of the present pipeline.
3.2. Historic map text detection and recognition
The extraction of text information from historic maps has received growing attention in parallel with advances in text detection and OCR. End-to-end map text reading systems have increasingly combined detection and recognition within a single learning framework, with dynamic text synthesis emerging as one response to the scarcity of labelled historic training data (Weinman et al., 2019).
More recent OCR progress has been driven by transformer architectures. TrOCR provides a pre-trained transformer-based recognition framework that can be adapted to OCR tasks (M. Li et al., 2021), while DTrOCR explores a decoder-only alternative for character recognition (Fujitake, 2023). For historic maps specifically, these models are most relevant not as general OCR benchmarks, but as evidence that recognition quality can be improved when sequence modelling is coupled with transfer learning.
At the pipeline level, systems such as MapKurator demonstrate that text detection, recognition, and linking can be integrated into a single workflow for cartographic materials (Kim et al., 2023). Synthetic data generation has also become an important supporting strategy for this area (Z. Li et al., 2021), and recent benchmark competitions have helped standardise evaluation across text detection, recognition, and linking subtasks (Li et al., 2024).
Current work on historic map text spotting increasingly emphasises robustness to scale variation, deformation, and collection-level processing. Hyper-local deformable transformers have been applied to text spotting on historic maps (Lin & Chiang, 2024), tightly coupled recognition pipelines have linked character-, word-, and place-level inference (Ray et al., 2019), and large-scale label extraction workflows have addressed the challenge of processing entire map collections rather than isolated sheets (Schlegel, 2021). Together, this literature supports the inclusion of a dedicated OCR and label-linking stage within an end-to-end historic map digitisation pipeline.
3.3. Road network vectorisation and graph construction
While deep learning approaches excel at identifying road pixels, they typically output probability maps or binary masks that require further processing before they become useful for transport network analysis.
Recent work on historic-map vectorisation has addressed parts of this gap, but not always in a way that yields graph structures suitable for network analysis. The framework of Jiao et al. (2024), for example, combines road vectorisation and classification through deep CNNs and symbol-painting functions tailored to cartographic road classes. Although that approach reports completeness scores as high as 90.69% for some classes, its primary emphasis remains geometric reconstruction and classification rather than the generation of topologically rigorous node-link graphs.
This distinction between geometric completeness and network usability has become more explicit in benchmark work on historical-map vectorisation. Standardised datasets and evaluation metrics introduced by Chen et al. (2024) highlight the trade-off between shape completeness and topological correctness, thereby reinforcing the need for topology-aware post-processing after segmentation. That need is central to Stage 2 of the present pipeline, where pixel predictions are converted into graph representations rather than retained as isolated polylines.
Large-scale historical road reconstruction has also been attempted by projecting historic evidence onto modern street databases. The probabilistic Road Overlap Indicator (ROI) approach reported by Uhl et al. (2022) achieved F1 scores up to 0.95 across more than 300,000 road segments, demonstrating the scalability of reference-assisted reconstruction. At the same time, reliance on the modern street network as the structural backbone limits the capacity to recover demolished roads, geometric shifts, or planned alignments that never persisted into the contemporary city.
3.4. Temporal network analysis and urban morphology
Spatial-network theory provides the conceptual foundation for interpreting reconstructed historic street graphs. Reviews of spatial networks emphasise how edge costs, planarity constraints, and betweenness centrality shape transport systems (Barthélemy, 2011), while early topological analyses of urban street networks formalised computational and functional graph representations and identified small-world structure in large street systems (Jiang & Claramunt, 2004). These ideas underpin the temporal network comparisons that the reconstructed networks are intended to support.
Longitudinal studies of urban growth have shown that street- network evolution is not random, but reflects identifiable structural processes. Nearly two centuries of network change north of Milan, for example, can be interpreted through the dual processes of densification around existing centres and exploration at the urban frontier (Strano et al., 2012). That work also showed that high-centrality roads tend to form persistent structural backbones across time, providing a useful theoretical basis for interpreting centrality patterns in reconstructed historic street networks.
Comparable longitudinal evidence from Greater London indicates that major spatial expansion can coexist with relatively stable average nodal connectivity, even as geometric and topological properties change over time (Masucci et al., 2013). A key limitation of that earlier literature, however, is its reliance on manually digitised historic networks; the present pipeline is designed to automate that reconstruction step and thereby extend longitudinal network analysis to larger map collections.
The contrast between organic growth and planned intervention has been especially clear in historical analyses of Paris, where Haussmann’s mid-nineteenth-century interventions redistributed betweenness centrality from radial corridors towards a more reticulated arterial structure (Barthélemy et al., 2013). That distinction provides a comparative lens for future analysis of Sydney’s colonial-era and post-expansion networks.
For evaluation, the closest methodological precedent is the semi-automated reconstruction framework of Turner et al. (2023), in which historic maps are geolocated onto modern street networks and contemporary geometry is reduced to fit historic representations. That dataset is used here as a validation benchmark, while the present pipeline extends the degree of automation beyond semi-manual geolocation and matching.
4. Data
4.1. Historic cartography: The John Sands collection
The primary dataset for this study comprises digitised plates from the John Sands’ Sydney and Suburban Directory, specifically the editions published in 1877 and 1903. These maps were selected for their cartographic standard and their coverage of Sydney’s rapid urban expansion period.
The maps were obtained from the City of Sydney Archives. The 1877 map, used primarily for training the U-Net segmentation model, covers the Sydney Central Business District (CBD) and immediate surrounds. The 1903 map (Fig. 1) (John Sands (Firm), 1903), used for the end-to-end pipeline evaluation, extends this coverage to include the expanding inner-city suburbs.

4.3. Ground truth and reference data
Ground truth and reference data were assembled from three sources, each serving a different role in the pipeline.
4.3.1. Manual annotations for training and intermediate validation
Pixel-level road masks were created through manual digitisation using EISeg (Efficient Interactive SEGmentation) (Hao et al., 2022), an interactive segmentation tool that accelerates annotation through click-based refinement. These masks provide the training and evaluation targets for the U-Net road extraction stage across both the Sydney maps and the additional held-out Melbourne test sheet.
For graph-level validation, manually digitised road graphs were created for the maps used in the Stage 2 comparison, including the 1877 John Sands map, the evaluated Sydney atlas sheets, and the Melbourne 1942 sheet. In addition, the 1903 map output was manually geolocated against reference data for transformation accuracy assessment.
4.3.2. Contemporary OpenStreetMap baseline
Contemporary road network data were extracted from OpenStreetMap (OSM) (Boeing, 2025) via the Overpass API (Export date: January 2026) and filtered to include only drivable roads, excluding tunnels and motorways that would not appear in historic surface maps. This dataset serves as the modern spatial baseline against which the geolocated historic graph is matched and from which the street and intersection dictionaries used in later stages are derived.
OSM represents the road network as it exists in 2026; as such, roads that were present previously but have since been demolished, pedestrianised, or absorbed into larger parcels will appear as false positives against this baseline, while modern infrastructure built after the tested maps will appear as false negatives.
An official cadastral or survey network would be positionally more accurate than OSM, but the difference is small compared with the registration residual of the hand-drawn source. Under automated geolocation that residual is about 120 m, around two orders of magnitude larger than any likely positional difference between OSM and an official product; with control points it falls to 4.3–30 m, and on the best-registered sheets an official product could make a measurable difference at a 5 m buffer. OSM was used because it is openly licensed and consistent across countries, which allows the pipeline to be applied to other cities and archives.
4.3.3. Historic reference network for end-to-end evaluation
End-to-end matching was also evaluated against the validated historic Sydney road network of Turner et al. (2023), which was produced through semi-automated georeferencing of historic maps onto modern street networks. This dataset provides a more appropriate historical reference for the 1903 evaluation than raw OSM alone because it partially decouples the historic network extent from the present-day street system.
The reference under-represents roads that left no modern geometric trace, and Section 5.8 sets out how this affects the reported precision and recall.

5.1.2. U-Net architecture and training
The road extraction process uses a U-Net encoder/decoder architecture (Ronneberger et al., 2015). The network uses symmetric encoder and decoder paths with skip connections to preserve spatial resolution during the segmentation process. The model is designed with spatial dropouts across all layers to minimise overfitting.
The segmentation model is trained on the John Sands 1877 map of Sydney (John Sands (Firm), 1877) using an online importance sampling strategy. Rather than using static pre-tiling, the source image and related ground truth are loaded into memory and sampled dynamically. Training patches of size 𝐻tile×𝑊tile
 are extracted at random coordinates, using a rejection sampling mechanism that prioritises the minority class, in this case the road network. This ensures patches contain road features, minimising class imbalance from the predominantly empty background. A sample of these patches can be seen in Fig. 3.
Data augmentation is then applied onto the extracted tiles at random using the Albumentations Python library (Buslaev et al., 2020), including rotation and horizontal and vertical flipping. Since this sampling has no natural epoch boundary, each epoch was capped at 𝑁epoch

5.2. Stage 2: Graph construction and vectorisation
5.2.1. Post-processing and binarisation
The probability map output from the road extraction requires post-processing to become usable as a road network. This is done by taking the raw probabilities and applying hysteresis thresholding, which assigns pixels into two groups: “High Threshold” being the group that has probability greater than or equal to 𝑇h
 of being in the road class, and “Low Threshold” containing those with probability between 𝑇low
 and 𝑇h
. These low-threshold pixels are then checked to see if they are connected to any pixels within the high-threshold group; if so the pixel is kept, otherwise it is removed. This reconnects broken roads caused by map artefacts, while still filtering low-probability noise.
The output then undergoes morphological cleaning using OpenCV (Bradski, 2000) operations with kernel 𝐾morph
. Morphological opening uses cv2.MORPH_OPEN to remove small noise artefacts and separate connected components that should be distinct, while morphological closing uses cv2.MORPH_CLOSE to fill small gaps within road segments and connect nearby road fragments.
5.2.2. Skeletonisation
The cleaned binary road map is converted to a skeletal representation using the skeletonise function of the scikit-image Python library (van der Walt et al., 2014), reducing thick road segments to single-pixel centrelines, preserving topology for graph conversion.

5.3. Stage 3: Street name detection and extraction
5.3.1. Text detection
The third stage focuses on extracting intersection and street identifiers for geolocation. A dictionary of intersections and roads within Sydney and Greater Sydney is compiled from OpenStreetMap (OSM) to refine the spatial allocation of the predictions.
To detect text within the maps, a DBNet++ (Differentiable Binarisation Network) architecture was implemented (Liao et al., 2022). Instead of treating text detection as a basic segmentation task, DBNet++ predicts the core area of the text (probability map) and the gradient boundaries around it (threshold map), allowing the model to separate closely printed or overlapping text.
As historic maps are too large to process at once, the model uses a sliding window approach to evaluate the image across multiple scales. The network outputs are then expanded into coordinate polygons to form bounding boxes around the text. Finally, a spatial index (R-tree) and a Union-Find algorithm are used to identify and merge overlapping polygons from the different scales into distinct, single text blocks.

5.3.3. OCR recognition
The pre-processed text images are processed using a trained OCR recognition model based on the TrOCR (Transformer OCR) framework (M. Li et al., 2021). This model has been trained on a custom dataset containing historic map text and human made predictions. As such the dataset includes period appropriate font styles, degradation artefacts, and variable text quality. The OCR predictions, including recognised text and confidence scores, are integrated back into the original JSON file alongside the spatial bounding box information.
5.4. Stage 4: Automated geolocation
Historic maps are hand-drawn documents subject to paper shrinkage, surveying error, and lithographic distortion; they are not precise geodetic representations. This motivates a non-rigid spatial transformation rather than a simple affine warp. One of the main challenges in historic digitisation is projecting a pixel-based map onto the real-world street network using map projection coordinates. In traditional geolocation, GCPs (Ground Control Points) are required for an accurate transformation. For historic maps these GCPs take the form of intersections, because intersections are uniquely named and identifiable using the data this pipeline has already extracted.
5.4.1. Name assignment
The name assignment process uses STRtree spatial indexing to check the relationship between OCR text polygons and road network links. 5.4.2. Name propagation algorithm
The name propagation algorithm uses a probabilistic flow model rather than strict rule-based assignment. Instead of binary decisions, the system assigns a confidence score (0.0 to 1.0) to every name on every road segment.
The process follows these key principles:
•
Angular Momentum: Names flow naturally along straight lines. When a named road enters an intersection, the algorithm calculates the viewing angle to all exiting roads.
•
Confidence Decay: As a name travels further from its source label, its confidence decays at rate 𝛼decay

•

5.4.8. Semi-automatic geolocation
Name-based anchoring fails when a sheet yields too few validated intersections to constrain a transform, which is the case on most of the smaller atlas sheets. For these sheets the pipeline provides a semi-automatic mode that replaces only the source of the correspondences and leaves the rest of the workflow unchanged.
An operator places a small number of ground control points on the raster and pairs each with its location in the modern network: between 2 and 13 points on the atlas sheets, 10 on Melbourne 1942 and 61 on the 1903 sheet. The system extends these points by junction matching between the manually digitised historic graph and the modern network, pairing intersections by proximity and connectivity, which gives between 76 and 3717 correspondences per sheet. Because the propagation uses the digitised graph rather than the Stage 1–2 prediction, the predicted network plays no part in its own registration.
A four-parameter similarity transform (uniform scale, rotation and translation) is fitted to these correspondences by least squares, mapping pixel coordinates (col,−row)
 to the projected modern coordinate system. A similarity transform is used instead of the affine or TPS models of the automated path because the correspondences are dense and spread across the sheet, so the residual is dominated by the survey and drafting error of the original map rather than by the flexibility of the model. The median residuals are 4.3–14.7 m on the atlas sheets, 17.2 m on Melbourne 1942 and 30.0 m on the 1903 sheet, compared with about 120 m from automated geolocation on the 1903 sheet.
This mode still requires an operator, but it reduces the task from tracing a network to placing a few points and removes the dependence of Stages 4–5 on OCR quality, which allows the thirteen sheets without enough validated OCR anchors to be carried through to matching.

5.7. Multi-objective hyperparameter optimisation
The performance of map matching algorithms is sensitive to the weights assigned to similarity metrics and the search thresholds used. Furthermore, map matching presents a classic multi-objective trade-off: relaxed thresholds often increase Recall (recovering more historic streets) at the expense of Precision (increasing false positives).
To address this, we used the Optuna framework (Akiba et al., 2019) to perform a multi-objective optimisation using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) (Deb et al., 2002). Rather than optimising for a single scalar metric (such as the F1-score), which can obscure trade-offs, NSGA-II seeks a Pareto Frontier of optimal solutions where no single objective can be improved without degrading another.

5.8. Evaluation metrics and ground truth
The primary evaluation compares the pipeline’s High and Medium confidence output against the historic Sydney road network of Turner et al. (2023), which records road segments with opening and closing dates derived from semi-automated georeferencing of historic maps. This reference is more suitable for a 1903 map than raw OSM because it records when roads opened and closed, although it was built on modern street geometry and so only partly separates the historic network from the present one. Melbourne 1942 lies outside that dataset and is evaluated against its manually digitised 1942 network. For each sheet the reference is clipped to the footprint of the full historic graph, so that the recall denominator does not depend on which links a configuration accepts.
Because the reference omits historic roads that left no modern trace, 1903 roads recovered by the pipeline can be scored as false positives, so the reported precision is a lower bound on true precision. The same omission has the opposite effect on recall: roads missing from the reference do not enter the recall denominator, which is therefore limited to roads that survive into the modern network. These are the roads Stage 5 can return, since its output lies on OSM edges, and a demolished road could not be recovered in any case, so the reported recall is optimistic with respect to the full 1903 network.
Spatial discrepancies between hand-drawn maps and modern baselines make one-to-one link matching insufficient. Instead, a Buffer-Intersection Method with a spatial tolerance of 𝜏buffer=5
 metres was used.
The geometry scored is the set of stitched OSM paths returned by Stage 5, not the warped historic graph. These paths lie on OSM edges, which is why a 5 m tolerance can be applied even though the registration residual is much larger, and the F1 therefore measures whether the pipeline selects the modern edges that correspond to the historic network rather than the positional accuracy of the reconstructed geometry, which is limited by the registration residual.

6.1. Stage 1: U-Net segmentation performance
The atlas sheets combine high precision (0.740–0.967) with low recall (0.506–0.565 on the held-out sheets and 0.425 on Waterloo, which is training data; Table 4), a pattern that indicates under-segmentation rather than border misclassification. Rasterising the predicted graph at increasing stroke widths against the annotated mask, with the geometry unchanged, raises recall from about 0.05 at one pixel to between 0.64 and 0.88 at fifteen pixels while precision stays between 0.79 and 0.96 (Table 5). 6.4. Stage 4: Geolocation and coordinate transformation results
Automated geolocation requires at least three non-collinear anchors for an affine fit, and enough anchors spread across the sheet to constrain the TPS warp. Because of their limited extent and accumulated propagation error, the smaller atlas sheets complete OCR and name propagation but retain too few usable anchors, with three of 216 candidate intersections on Annandale, three of 308 on Macdonaldtown and none of 231 on Waterloo, against 33 of 441 on the 1903 sheet. The 1903 sheet, which covers inner Sydney with a dense and well-labelled network, is therefore the only sheet evaluated with fully automated geolocation, and the other sheets use the semi-automatic mode of Section 5.4.8.
6.4.1. Anchor point processing pipeline
The initial extraction process yielded 441 potential intersection points.
•
Geographic Filtering: A boundary validation step removed gross outliers, retaining 415 validated intersections within the target geographic area.
•
Anchor Point Distribution: The candidate anchors are spread across the map (Fig. 9a), which limits geometric distortion in the transformation.
RANSAC retains 33 inliers (Fig. 9b), whose convex hull is wide enough to constrain the TPS warp while the noisiest OCR matches are excluded. Varying the inlier threshold from 0.0025 to 0.04 changes the median F1 at a 50 m buffer by 0.03 across three RANSAC draws, so the rejection rate reflects the configured threshold rather than the quality of the anchors.

6.4.2. Network transformation validation
Following the isolation of the 33 inlier anchor points, an initial affine transformation was calculated to globally align the historic map with modern coordinates. The subsequent non-linear TPS warping reduced the mean error from 2634 m to 120 m and the maximum error from 4143 m to 252 m. This accuracy is acceptable given the inherent geometric limitations of 19th-century hand-drawn cartography noted in Section 5.
In the re-run on the 18-inlier anchor set of Table 9, the coarse alignment of Section 5.4.5 alone leaves a mean error of 1286 m, a least-squares affine fit 113 m and the TPS warp 103 m, with maxima of 2533 m, 315 m and 292 m. Most of the reduction therefore comes from the affine fit rather than the TPS warp, and the 2634 m starting error of the original run is closer in magnitude to the coarse alignment stage than to an affine fit.
6.5. Stage 5: Similarity matching
The Stage 5 parameters were selected with the NSGA-II search of Section 5.7, using 220 trials to explore the trade-off between recall and precision. Under fully automated geolocation with the original Stage 5 configuration, Stage 5 processed 21,103 historic links on the 1903 sheet, accepted 20,304 and traversed 24,093 OSM links, for an F1 of 0.668 against (Turner et al., 2023).
6.5.1. Pareto frontier analysis
The best recall found rises from 0.550 in the opening trials to 0.576 by trial 52, and the remaining 168 trials add less than 0.001, which is within the run-to-run variation of the matcher (Section 5.8); the plateau therefore reflects the resolution of the search as much as a fully explored parameter space. The Pareto frontier in Fig. 10 shows the inverse relationship between recall and precision:
•

7. Discussion and conclusion
7.1. Semantic anchoring for automated geolocation
Manual ground control points and visual feature matching both depend on finding the same features on the historic and modern maps, which becomes harder as urban form diverges, whereas street names read by OCR identify intersections directly. On the 1903 sheet RANSAC retained 33 of the 415 candidate anchors, which shows that OCR-based name matching remains noisy, although the retained anchors were enough to register the sheet with a mean error of 120 m.
7.2. Segmentation robustness and generalisation
The U-Net’s recall of 0.971 on the 1903 sheet (Table 4) shows that it still finds the roads on a map drawn 26 years after its training sheet, and the graph F1 of 0.860 to 0.882 on the held-out atlas sheets (Table 6) shows that Stage 2 holds on a different map series. Melbourne 1942 extends both results to a second city, and the semi-automatic geolocation of Section 5.4.8 carries it through Stages 4–5; the name-based anchoring of Stage 4 has been tested only on Sydney.
The U-Net’s precision falls from 0.876 on the 1877 sheet to 0.766 on the 1903 sheet, so the transfer still depends on map style. Changes in lithography, degradation and symbols lead the model to over-predict road-like features, and the 1877 training data therefore support partial transfer between styles but not the robustness needed for mixed historic collections.
7.3. Network matching and historic divergence
The F1 scores of 0.573 to 0.724 on the Sydney atlas sheets and 0.651 on the 1903 sheet (Table 11) are not classification accuracy against a complete 1903 network, because the Turner et al. (2023) reference contains only historic roads with a modern counterpart; as set out in Section 5.8, the reported precision is therefore a lower bound and the reported recall is optimistic. The Pareto frontier in Fig. 10 shows the cost of stricter geometric criteria, which raise precision by rejecting roads that are correct but distorted by nineteenth-century surveying and map production.
The conditional recall of 0.974 in the four-cell classification of Section 6.5.3 shows that the matcher finds almost every historic link with a modern road nearby, but the classification measures proximity rather than survival: in a dense grid a modern road is almost always nearby, and showing that an individual road survived would require name matching or topological correspondence.
7.4. Computing requirements and time
The pipeline runs on consumer hardware, with OCR accounting for most of the runtime. On an M4 Max MacBook Pro with 36 GB of memory, road extraction, vectorisation and text detection take under 30 s on the suburb sheets. On the 1903 sheet, the largest in the study at 23,348×35,346
 pixels, a full Stages 1–4 run took 10.6 h, of which OCR took 9.5 h for 20,661 detected text elements, text detection 6.6 min and geolocation 76 s. OCR therefore takes more than eight times as long as the rest of the pipeline combined and is the stage that limits city-scale processing, while the suburb sheets, with one to two orders of magnitude fewer text elements, complete in minutes.
7.5. Limitations
The main limitation of the current pipeline is not segmentation or graph extraction, but automated geolocation. Stable registration depends on obtaining enough correctly detected and recognised named intersections, which in turn depends on OCR quality and on the persistence of comparable street structure in the contemporary reference network. Large maps can absorb OCR noise through anchor abundance; smaller maps often cannot.
On the suburb sheets, which retained too few validated anchors for automated registration (Section 6.4), Stage 5 under fully automated geolocation accepted 6 of 405 links on Annandale and none on Macdonaldtown or Waterloo. The semi-automatic mode of Section 5.4.8 addresses this limitation: with seven control points, the Macdonaldtown graph that produced no accepted links yields 278 accepted links at F1 0.638, which shows that the failure on this sheet was in geolocation rather than matching, and that where few anchors can be validated a small amount of manual input recovers the sheet while keeping most of the labour saving of the pipeline.
In dense urban areas such as the Sydney CBD, where roads, labels and linework are tightly packed, segmentation and graph construction errors compound, because merged road predictions arise where road boundaries cannot be separated and skeletonisation carries these errors into the graph topology.
Performance is also likely to decline on maps with different road symbology, heavier text overlay, unconventional orientation or more severe damage, so the pipeline should be regarded as reliable within the range of map styles tested here rather than across historic cartography in general.
7.6. Future research directions
Three priorities follow directly from these results. The first is improvement in OCR and text validation, since semantic anchoring is currently the main constraint on automated geolocation; this includes the street dictionary, because a street renamed since the map was drawn loses its anchor in the same way as a misread label, and period gazetteers such as the street indexes in the John Sands directories could supply the historic names that a modern dictionary lacks. The second is stronger robustness to domain shift through broader training data and style-aware augmentation across multiple cartographic sources. The third is extension to additional map series and cities, so that the present pipeline can be tested at larger historical and geographic scales. A separate line of future work is the application of these reconstructed networks to longitudinal urban-network analysis, which is beyond the scope of the present paper and will be developed separately.
The same framework could also be extended beyond transport networks. Building footprints, land-use boundaries, and other topographic features would not only broaden the historical information recoverable from scanned maps, but may also improve geolocation and interpretation by providing additional semantic and geometric constraints.
7.7. Conclusion
Historic street maps can therefore be treated as analysable network sources rather than as purely visual historical artefacts. By combining deep learning segmentation, graph construction, OCR-based semantic anchoring, and similarity-based matching in a unified workflow, the present study shows that georeferenced road graphs can be recovered from scanned historic maps with limited manual intervention, with fourteen maps from two cities and three map series carried through to matched networks using between 2 and 61 manually placed control points per map.
